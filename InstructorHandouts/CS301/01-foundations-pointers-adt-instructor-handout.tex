\section{Before You Teach This Lecture}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Wirth, \textit{Algorithms and Data Structures}, \S4.2, p.111} --- the pointer / dynamic-allocation anchor. \S4.1 (p.109) gives the recursive-variability motivation. \emph{Page-cited}; index built 2026-08-11. Read the \S4.2 opening paragraph if you only read one thing.
  \item \textbf{Sedgewick \& Wayne, \textit{Algorithms}, \S1.2, p.64} --- the ADT/data-type definition; \S1.3, p.120 --- the bag as a collection type; \S1.4, p.172 --- the ``how long will my program take'' motivation for cost analysis. \emph{Page-cited}. All are in Java; convert syntax to C per the notation registry, never present it as C.
  \item \textbf{Karumanchi, \textit{Data Structures And Algorithms Made Easy}, Ch.~1, p.18--19 (PDF)} --- the ADT definition (``a declaration of data'' plus ``a declaration of operations'') and its list of commonly used ADTs, used here to preview the Weeks~3--12 roadmap. \emph{Page-cited}; index built 2026-08-11. \textbf{Not} a source for this lecture's pointer/\texttt{malloc} content --- indexing found zero coverage of structured programming or \texttt{malloc}/\texttt{calloc}/\texttt{realloc}/\texttt{free} in this book.
  \item \textbf{Horowitz \& Sahni, \textit{Fundamentals of Data Structures}, Ch.~1} and \textbf{Aho--Hopcroft--Ullman, Ch.~1} --- \emph{not page-indexed}. Cite at chapter level for structured programming / the ADT framing.
  \item \textbf{Scope notice:} this lecture deliberately defers \texttt{calloc} and \texttt{realloc}. Allocation vocabulary is only \texttt{malloc}, \texttt{sizeof}, \texttt{free}, \texttt{NULL}. Do not introduce the other two here even if students ask --- it dilutes the one-block story.
  \item \textbf{Plan the board examples in advance} (see each section below, marked \textbf{Board:}). The two running threads --- the stack trace and the heap trace --- are the ones to rehearse; the numbers on the board must be right the first time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Starting Point: Three Questions}

Open by stating the question the whole lecture answers: \emph{where does a running C program keep its data?} Write it at the top of the board and leave it there --- every section of this lesson is one corner of that answer.

Pose the three questions on the board before revealing anything: (1) where does a running C program keep its data, (2) how can we use memory we request while the program runs, (3) how can we describe a data structure before implementing it. Let students guess before you structure them.

\textbf{Board: three quick prediction boxes.} Draw three labelled boxes (``where,'' ``how,'' ``describe'') and invite one-word answers from the class; leave the boxes unfilled until the end and return to them in the summary. This is the lesson's live spine and costs nothing.

\begin{teachingaside}
The trap to avoid on the first frame is jumping straight into vocabulary. The ``A Variable Has a Place'' idea --- \texttt{int marks = 80} has a name, a value, \emph{and} an address --- is the seed of everything; spend the time making the address concrete before naming the stack. Use the house-number line: ``an address is like a house number; it tells us where to look.''
\end{teachingaside}

\textbf{Board: a variable and its place.} Draw one box for \texttt{marks}, put \texttt{80} inside it, and write an invented address (say \texttt{0x7FFC}) above the box. Ask: ``if we only knew the value, could we tell the box from its neighbour? What else do we need?'' Answer: the address --- which is exactly what a pointer stores.

\section{Motivation and the Overall Syllabus (new Act 0)}

This rebuilt deck opens with a short Act 0 that answers \emph{why} before \emph{what}. Its job is motivational, not technical --- nothing here is examined directly, but it sets the frame the rest of the term leans on.

\textbf{Motivation: correct is not always fast enough.} Use the student-record story from the Notes: a loop that scans records is correct at 5 records and unusable at 10 million. The point to land: \emph{the answer is the same; the arrangement of the data is the difference}. This is the single sentence that justifies the whole course. \textbf{Board:} write ``5 records --- instant'' and ``10{,}000{,}000 records, a query every millisecond --- unusable'' on two lines, then draw an arrow between them labelled ``same correct loop.'' Ask: ``what changed?'' --- not the loop, the arrangement.

\textbf{Motivation: ``it felt fast'' is not an argument.} Sedgewick's \S1.4 opener (p.172) poses the two questions ``how long will my program take?'' and ``why does my program run out of memory?'' as too vague to answer precisely without a model. Land the contrast: Week 1 gives the raw material (memory layout, pointers), Week 2 gives the model (counting operations, $O$/$\Omega$/$\Theta$). This frame makes Week 2 feel like the payoff of Week 1 rather than a new topic.

\textbf{The course in one roadmap.} Walk the six clusters: Weeks 1--2 foundations; 3--5 linear structures (arrays, stacks, queues, expression processing); 6--7 linked structures; 8--9 searching and sorting; 10 files and hashing; 11--12 trees, BSTs, AVL, graphs, MSTs, shortest paths. Emphasise the recurring shape: \emph{state the contract, then implement it, then analyse what it costs} --- the same three beats every week repeats.

\begin{teachingaside}
Students commonly ask ``why are we learning memory stuff in a data-structures course?'' The roadmap slide is the honest answer: every later structure is a claim about \emph{where data lives and how it is reached}, so the memory model is not a prerequisite --- it is the subject. Keep this slide to under two minutes and come back to it at the Week 1 summary.
\end{teachingaside}

\textbf{The running thread: the expression processor.} Introduce the small program that reads \texttt{(a+b)*c} and must check its brackets, convert it, evaluate it, and look up its identifiers. Map each future week onto a deficiency it fixes: a stack checks the brackets (Weeks 3--4); a symbol table, first a list (Week 6) then a hash table (Week 10), stores the identifiers; an expression tree evaluates it (Week 11). This is the same ``one program learning new tricks'' thread the Week 0 deck previewed; state it once here and it pays off all term.

\textbf{What you already know.} The bridge slide: variables, arrays, functions, loops, \texttt{struct} --- all from prior courses. The single new thing this course adds is the question ``what arrangement of data fits this job?'' and the discipline of answering it with a contract, an implementation, and a cost.

\subsection{The stack grows (Questions worth the whole story)}

The deck's step-frames (Figures~\ref{fig:stack1}--\ref{fig:stack2}) make automatic storage a three-step story:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{main} starts $\Rightarrow$ one frame holding \texttt{int a = 5;}.
  \item \texttt{add(x=5, y=3)} is called $\Rightarrow$ a second frame is pushed \emph{below}, holding the parameters and \texttt{int s = x + y; = 8}.
  \item \texttt{add} returns $\Rightarrow$ its frame is popped, and \texttt{8} was copied back into \texttt{main}'s frame.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Board: live stack trace (interactive).} Draw the empty board frame; ask a student to ``call'' \texttt{add} by walking to the board and pushing a new frame below \texttt{main}'s. Then a second student ``returns'' and erases it, copying \texttt{8} up. Two volunteers, three steps, and the whole class watches the stack grown and shrink. Ask mid-way: ``\emph{where does the next frame go --- higher or lower address?}'' (Lower --- the stack grows down.)

\textbf{Board: a second, distinct trace (prediction trap).} Pose a different call: \texttt{main} holds \texttt{int n = 3;} and calls \texttt{doubleIt(n)} whose local is \texttt{int d = 2*n;}. Ask the class to draw the two-frame picture \emph{before} you do, including which address region is higher. Reveal after a show of hands.

\begin{teachingaside}
The recurring student error here is treating the stack as a ``magic box that grows.'' The teaching move is the low-address line: the \emph{directions} (heap up, stack down) get drawn once and reused for 12 weeks; if you vary them you undo a later diagram. Also be explicit that \emph{automatic} means ``the program manages it with no instructions from you'' --- students conflate ``automatic'' with ``I don't have to think about it,'' which is exactly wrong when pointers enter.
\end{teachingaside}

\subsection{Why the stack is not enough}

State the requirement the stack cannot meet: the user enters the record count only after the program runs, and we may need 3 records today, 300 tomorrow. The stack decides frame sizes at compile time and discards locals on return --- it fails on size \emph{and} lifetime.

\textbf{Board: the guessing trap.} Write \texttt{int a[1000000];} on the board and ask: ``when is this wasteful, and when does it still fail?'' (Wasteful when the count is small; fails when it exceeds a million --- so guessing rescues nothing.) This reuses Wirth's p.111 argument without the formalism: variable-sized data needs storage allocated \emph{when the data comes into existence} \cite{Wirth2004_algorithms_data_structures}.

\textbf{Board: the prediction question (live).} Put the two declarations on the board:
\begin{center}
\texttt{int score = 80;} \quad vs. \quad \texttt{int *p = malloc(sizeof(int));}
\end{center}
Ask: ``\emph{which variable can outlive the function that created it?}'' Let votes land. Answer: the \emph{block}, not the variable that names it --- the pointer is only the handle. This sets up the entire next two sections. Do not reveal \texttt{malloc} detail here; you told them the call exists, and that is the hook.

\section{Pointers: The Handle on Memory}

\begin{definitionbox}{Pointer}
A variable that stores an address. Its type records what kind of object resides at that address \cite{Wirth2004_algorithms_data_structures}.
\end{definitionbox}

The contrast to draw: an ordinary variable stores a value such as \texttt{80}; a pointer stores a \emph{place}. \texttt{int *p;} means ``\texttt{p} can point to an \texttt{int}.'' Every pointer is a handle, not the data.

  lives at that address.}

\textbf{Board: box-and-arrow.} Draw the two boxes, put \texttt{80} in the right one, then draw the arrow and label it ``points to.'' Ask: ``\emph{what does \texttt{p} contain --- the \texttt{80} or the address?}'' (The address.) The arrow is the whole abstraction.

\subsection{Pointers reach structures and stop}

Introduce the two-field node:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
struct node {
  int data;
  struct node *next;
};
\end{verbatim}
\end{center}

\texttt{data} stores the value; \texttt{next} stores the address of \emph{another} node; if there is no next node, \texttt{next = NULL}. Establish the arrow field-access now: \texttt{p->data} reads the \texttt{data} field of the node \texttt{p} points at. (The deck defines \texttt{->} at first use.)

\begin{definitionbox}{\texttt{NULL}}
The address value meaning ``points at nothing.'' Reading through it is undefined behaviour --- in practice, a crash.
\end{definitionbox}

\textbf{Board: node-picture drill (interactive).} Draw three boxes --- \texttt{head} on the board's left, then two node boxes, \texttt{data}/\texttt{next} each. Ask the class to draw the arrows so that \texttt{head} points at node 1 and node 1 points at node 2, with node 2's \texttt{next} written \texttt{NULL}. Then erase the middle arrow and ask: ``\emph{what happens to node 2 now?}'' Answer: reachable no longer --- a preview of how a \bad{dangling} situation begins.

\textbf{Board: second distinct instance.} Instead of node 2, ask the class to extend to a third node and mark where the chain terminates --- then give the one-line meaning of \texttt{struct node *head = NULL;} (``the list is empty''). Two arrows, labels, one \texttt{NULL}: a cheap repeat that fixes the convention.

\begin{teachingaside}
Students will write \texttt{((*p).data)} from an earlier course and will resist \texttt{p->data} as ``sugar.'' Hold the line: the course standard is \texttt{->} everywhere, and the convention registry enforces it. Also expect the ``is a pointer a number?'' question --- yes, an address is a number, but its \emph{type} records what lives there; that is why \texttt{int *} and \texttt{struct node *} are different types.
\end{teachingaside}

\section{One Heap Block: Allocate, Use, Return}

The whole memory discipline of the course is three moves, done to one block at a time. Restate the lesson's spine before this section: \good{request memory, use it, return it.} Section~\ref{sec:heap-detail} below is the worked core; Section~\ref{sec:heap-growth} is the growth picture; Section~\ref{sec:heap-return} is the return and the two mistakes.

\section{Requesting One Integer}
\label{sec:heap-detail}

State the pattern before any explanation:

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
int *p = malloc(sizeof(int));
if (p == NULL) return 1;
*p = 80;
\end{verbatim}
}

Three facts: \texttt{malloc} requests bytes from the heap; \texttt{sizeof(int)} asks C for the right number of bytes; \texttt{p} stores the address of the requested space. Emphasise that you never count bytes by hand.

\textbf{Board: the pattern with a distinct instance.} Walk the same three lines but for \texttt{double} instead of \texttt{int}: \texttt{double *d = malloc(sizeof(double)); if (d == NULL) return 1; *d = 3.5;}. Ask: ``\emph{what changes between the int and the double version?}'' Answer: only the type in the pointer declaration and inside \texttt{sizeof} --- the shape is identical. This is the ``one pattern, many types'' payoff.

\textbf{Board: live failure trace.} Ask ``\emph{which line would fail if \texttt{p} is \texttt{NULL}?}'' (Line three, the dereference.) If \texttt{malloc} returned \texttt{NULL}, line three reads from nothing --- a crash. The \texttt{if} guard makes line two the controlled exit. Correct the habit live: never let a line touch a pointer that \emph{may} be \texttt{NULL} before you have checked.

\begin{teachingaside}
The most common lab mistake this week is dropping the \texttt{if (p == NULL)} line ``because it never happens.'' The teaching move is the trace above: the dereference is not protected, so an out-of-memory condition becomes a crash at a random later line. And do not let students \texttt{malloc(sizeof(8))}; the argument is the \emph{type}, and \texttt{sizeof} is the type-size operator.
\end{teachingaside}

\subsection{Allocate a complete node}

The safer engineering pattern for the whole course:

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
struct node *p = malloc(sizeof(struct node));
if (p == NULL) return 1;
p->data = 42;
p->next = NULL;
\end{verbatim}
}

Four properties to narrate: \texttt{sizeof(struct node)} asks for exactly one node (not a hand-counted \texttt{8} or \texttt{12}); the result is checked before use; \texttt{p->data} sets the value field; \texttt{p->next = NULL} marks the explicit end. Initialising every field makes state predictable.

\textbf{Board: audit-the-node checklist (interactive ``code review'').} Write the four checks and ask the class to \emph{vet a snippet you show}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Was the allocation checked against \texttt{NULL}?
  \item Was every field initialised before use?
  \item Is there exactly one clear owner responsible for \texttt{free(p)}?
  \item Can the program reach the node later, or has its pointer been lost?
\end{enumerate}
Show a deliberately broken variant --- say, no \texttt{NULL} check and no \texttt{next = NULL} --- and have students find the two violations. Then fix it live.

\textbf{Board: trace-before-run challenge.} Put the two lines \texttt{int *p = malloc(sizeof(int)); *p = 80;} on the board and ask: ``\emph{what is true after these lines?}'' (p holds an address; the block holds \texttt{80}; \texttt{p} is not the block.) Reuse Figure~\ref{fig:happened} as the shared reference.

\section{How the Heap Grows}
\label{sec:heap-growth}

Now the converse picture: instead of growing the stack downward, the heap grows \emph{upward}. Three steps, mirroring the stack trace (Figures~\ref{fig:heap1}--\ref{fig:heap3}).

\textbf{Board: heap growth trace (interactive).} Draw the empty heap column. Ask a volunteer to ``call \texttt{malloc(3 * sizeof(int))}'' and draw the block holding \texttt{10 20 30} just above the bottom; a second volunteer \texttt{malloc}'s \texttt{q}'s two-element block \emph{above} it; a third volunteers \texttt{free(p)} and shades the returned region ``free again.'' The class watches the used heap grow up then shrink.

\begin{teachingaside}
The single most instructive contrast in the lecture is running the two traces back to back: a volunteer pushed a stack frame \emph{down} and a volunteer pushed a heap block \emph{up}. Ask ``\emph{why opposite directions?}'' --- so the two regions can grow toward each other and share one free space without colliding (the real layout). If time permits, say only that much; the deck says no more in Week 1.
\end{teachingaside}

\textbf{Board: second distinct instance (prediction).} Cover Steps 2--3 and ask: ``\emph{after \texttt{free(q)}, where does a new \texttt{malloc} of three integers land?}'' Let guesses land, then reveal that exact placement is the allocator's choice --- what matters is that freed space is \emph{reusable}, not its guessed address. This inoculates students against hard-coding addresses.

\section{Returning the Memory, and Two Beginner Mistakes}
\label{sec:heap-return}

The return idiom is two lines that travel together:

{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
free(p);
p = NULL;
\end{verbatim}
}

\texttt{free(p)} gives the heap block back to the system; \texttt{p = NULL} makes the old pointer visibly empty so it cannot be mistakenly used. This is the top of the deck's summary line: \good{Request memory, use it, return it.}

The two mistakes that violate it:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \bad{Memory leak}: forget to call \texttt{free}; the block stays reserved (and if the pointer is overwritten, nothing can reach it ever again). Nothing crashes; the program quietly grows.
  \item \bad{Dangling pointer}: use a pointer after its block was freed. Reads are garbage; writes corrupt whatever now occupies that space.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Board: the two mistakes side by side (interactive clinical case).} Draw the heap with one block; then:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Sketch the \bad{leak}: erase the variable box while leaving the heap block --- ask ``\emph{who can still reach this block?}'' (No one. It is gone from reach but still reserved.)
  \item Sketch the \bad{dangling} pointer: shade the freed block ``free,'' keep the pointer arrow sharp --- ask ``\emph{what does the arrow now point at?}'' (Nothing usable.)
\end{enumerate}
The live habit: the owner is the one who frees. Ask a student to re-state who owns the node allocated in the \texttt{struct node} example.

\textbf{Board: a second, distinct instance.} Show the leak on a \emph{loop}: a program that allocates one node per iteration and never frees any. Trace the growth and ask: ``\emph{what eventually happens to the program?}'' (It runs out of memory; nothing crashes today, but it dies on a long run.) This makes the leak concrete rather than abstract.

\begin{teachingaside}
Expect students to free \emph{without} setting \texttt{NULL}, then to dereference the stale pointer in a later function and blame the compiler. The pair \texttt{free(p); p = NULL;} is a single habit --- teach it as one gesture, not two lines. Also expect the ``I freed it but nothing bad happened'' shrug: the bug is silent today and fatal on the next run. That silence is why the framework exists.
\end{teachingaside}

\section{Simple Contracts: The ADT}

\begin{definitionbox}{Abstract data type (ADT)}
A description of what we can do with data, without saying how it is stored: a promise about operations \cite{Sedgewick2011_algorithms}.
\end{definitionbox}

The contract states \emph{what}; the implementation states \emph{how}; different implementations can follow the same promise. Sedgewick \& Wayne's one-liner, p.64: a data type is ``a set of values and a set of operations on those values'' \cite{Sedgewick2011_algorithms}.

\textbf{Board: the bag contract (write it first).} Write on the board, one line each:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{insert(x)} --- put \texttt{x} into the bag.
  \item \texttt{member(x)} --- answer whether \texttt{x} is present.
  \item \texttt{size()} --- report how many items are present.
\end{enumerate}
Pause and underline: ``\emph{the contract does not say whether the bag uses an array or a list.}'' That silence is the feature.

\textbf{Board: one contract, two implementations (interactive classify).} Draw the contract box on top, then two boxes below --- \emph{dynamic array} and \emph{linked nodes} --- with ``implements'' arrows (Figure~\ref{fig:adt}). Run the deck's classify drill live, having students raise hands:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item ``\texttt{member(7)} answers yes or no.'' --- \good{contract}: what users can ask.
  \item ``The values are stored in an array.'' --- \bad{implementation}: how.
  \item ``\texttt{size()} reports the number of values.'' --- \good{contract}: what users can ask.
\end{enumerate}
The split is the lasting skill: \key{state the contract before writing a line of the implementation}.

\textbf{Board: second distinct instance (design question).} Pose a variant contract --- ``a queue of integers with \texttt{enqueue} and \texttt{dequeue}'' --- and ask the class which of the two bottom boxes they would \emph{guess} is cheaper for \emph{dequeue}. (Either answer is fine; the point is that both \emph{could} implement it, which is the abstract claim.) Note Sedgewick \& Wayne's \S1.3, p.120 offers bag/queue/stack as three collections differing only in which object is removed next --- a one-minute pointer for the curious.

\textbf{Board: the course roadmap, ADT by ADT.} List on the board: Linked Lists, Stacks, Queues, Priority Queues, Binary Trees, Disjoint Sets, Hash Tables, Graphs --- the commonly used ADTs, in Karumanchi's phrasing, ``a declaration of data'' plus ``a declaration of operations'' (Karumanchi2017, p.~18--19 PDF) \cite{Karumanchi2017_data_structures_algorithms_made_easy}. Point out that this is not a random list: it is exactly the Weeks~3--12 work-list, and every one of those chapters opens the same way this section just did --- ``What is a Stack?'', then ``Stack ADT'', \emph{then} implementation. Today's bag is the class's first rehearsal of a pattern the course repeats eight more times.

\section{Synthesis and Summary}

Close by returning to the three prediction boxes from the opening frame and filling them in: \emph{where} = the stack (short-lived locals) and the heap (run-time requests); \emph{how} = \texttt{malloc}/\texttt{sizeof} obtains a heap block and \texttt{free} returns it, through a pointer handle; \emph{describe} = an ADT states operations before any implementation. The three boxes are the three questions of the title slide, now answered.

\textbf{Quick reference.} \emph{Stack:} automatic, scope-bound, grows down; each call pushes a frame, each return pops it. \emph{Heap:} run-time sized, lifetime-controlled, grows up; \texttt{malloc} obtains, \texttt{free} returns, \texttt{NULL} means ``nothing.'' \emph{Pointer:} stores an address; \texttt{->} reads a field; never read through \texttt{NULL}. \emph{Allocation idiom:} \texttt{malloc} + \texttt{sizeof} $\rightarrow$ check \texttt{NULL} $\rightarrow$ initialise every field $\rightarrow$ use $\rightarrow$ \texttt{free} + set \texttt{NULL}. \emph{Two mistakes:} leak (never freed) and dangling pointer (used after free); every allocation has one owner. \emph{ADT:} operations stated without storage; a data structure is one implementation.

Set up Week 2's hook: we can now build two implementations of the same bag, but we have no way to say which is better --- only ``it felt fast.'' Next week supplies the vocabulary (asymptotic analysis) to compare them without running either.
